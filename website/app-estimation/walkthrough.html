Background

Why estimation, in two minutes

A significance test answers one narrow question: could a difference this large arise by chance if there were truly no difference? A p value says nothing about how big the difference is or how precisely it is known. Estimation statistics turn the picture around: report the size of the effect, in the units of the outcome, with an interval that shows how precisely the data pin it down. The p value is still there, but it stops being the headline.

The tools in this walkthrough follow that stance. The mean difference with its 95% confidence interval is the estimate. The sampling distribution pictures how plausible each value is, not just the two ends of an interval. The Difference plot puts the difference on the same panel as the data so it can be read directly instead of inferred from two error bars. The ROPE asks the question researchers actually care about: is the difference big enough to matter? Every number comes from the same test the chart's brackets already use (Welch's t on Compare Groups, the paired t on Repeated Measures), so the chart, the brackets and the Statistics panel can never disagree.

A convention to keep in mind: a difference is always the named category minus the reference. "Low dose minus Control = +1.5" means Low dose scored 1.5 g higher.

Tier 1

The difference with its interval, on brackets and in the Statistics panel

What it means

The mean difference is the first mean minus the second, in the data's own units. Its 95% confidence interval is the range of true differences the data are consistent with, from the same t test as the p value. An interval that excludes 0 corresponds to p below .05 for a two-sided test; an interval that includes 0 does not.

Why use it

"p = .003" tells a reader nothing about size. "Low dose raised weight by 1.5 g, 95% CI [-0.8, 3.9]" tells them the size, the direction and the uncertainty in one breath, which is what journals now ask for and what a reader can act on.

  1. Open the demo link, click Open, and select the chart tab Both on bars, with a ROPE. Click the + button in the chart toolbar (its tooltip says Add to chart) and pick Sig. bracket.

    Expect: a bracket appears over two bars with an asterisk label, and its panel opens.

  2. In the bracket panel, open its Stats tab and set Label to Difference with CI.

    Expect: the bracket label reads like Δ = 4.15, 95% CI [1.6, 6.7] (High dose vs Control) or the pair the bracket spans. The readout box below the select shows the test (Welch's t) and the same numbers.

  3. Click the Σ button in the toolbar (Statistics). On the Compare pairs tab, find the Difference and 95% CI columns.

    Expect: one row per pair (Control vs Low dose, Control vs High dose, Low dose vs High dose). The Difference for a row equals the first category's mean minus the second's, and its interval matches the bracket label for the same pair (sign flipped if the bracket reads the other way round).

  4. Click the Difference column header, then a value in that column.

    Expect: a small popover defines the term; the value's popover adds an "In this table" reading with that row's own numbers ("the first group named minus the second").

  5. Click a row's APA button and paste the result somewhere (a note, a document).

    Expect: the sentence ends with the estimate, for example ..., Δ = 4.15, 95% CI [1.6, 6.7]. In Word or Docs the statistics are italicized.

  6. Still in Compare pairs, open the ▾ beside Place brackets and choose Difference with CI, tick a pair, and press Place brackets.

    Expect: the placed bracket carries the difference-with-interval label; brackets placed earlier with this style relabel too.

Tier 2

Sampling distributions (the cat's eye)

What it means

Beside each mean, the shape is the t sampling distribution of that mean, scaled by its standard error (sd divided by the square root of n). It is widest at the mean and thins away from it: the width at any value says how plausible that value is for the population mean. The 95% CI error bar is cut from this same curve, so the two always agree.

Why use it

An error bar makes the interval look flat, as if every value inside it were equally plausible and every value outside impossible. The shape corrects both misreadings at a glance: the middle is far more plausible than the edges, and plausibility fades rather than stopping. It is the picture Cumming's estimation texts use to teach what a CI is.

  1. Select the chart tab Sampling distributions.

    Expect: each of the three bars carries a translucent shape to the right of its error bar, widest at the bar top, sitting on a white base so it reads the same above and below the bar.

  2. Compare the shape's height with the error bar (95% CI on this chart).

    Expect: the error bar's ends fall where the shape has already thinned to a sliver; the shape extends a little past them. Hover a shape for its tooltip (the mean, SE, n and df).

  3. Click a shape. In its panel, open Shape and switch from Half eye to Eye, then back; drag the width slider.

    Expect: Eye mirrors the shape around the mean (the classic cat's eye); Half eye puts the whole width to one side with its flat edge on the mean. The width follows the slider live.

  4. Open Color: turn Follow graph colors off and pick a color; then Opacity.

    Expect: all three shapes take the custom color (one color for the overlay); opacity changes live. Turning Follow graph colors back on returns each shape to its bar's color.

  5. Use the panel title bar's eye to hide the shapes, then the toolbar eye (Visibility) to bring them back. Use the title bar's trash to remove the overlay and the + menu to add it again.

    Expect: hide keeps the setting and lists the overlay as hidden in the Visibility panel; trash removes it outright; the + menu's Sampling distribution item turns it back on (and simply opens its panel when it is already on).

  6. Switch the chart type (toolbar, left) to Dot, then to Line, then back to Bar.

    Expect: on dot and line charts the shape sits beside the marker with no white base, so the connecting line stays visible through it. Nothing else changes.

Tier 3

The Difference plot

What it means

After the last category, one slot per other category shows that category's mean minus a reference category's mean: a triangle at the difference, a heavier line for its 95% CI, and a half eye for its sampling distribution. They read against a difference axis on the right whose zero sits level with the reference mean, so each triangle also sits exactly at its own category's mean. Dotted guides make both facts visible.

Why use it

This is the Gardner-Altman estimation plot: the effect and its uncertainty on the same picture as the data, in the data's units. A reader no longer has to judge whether two error bars "overlap enough"; the difference is drawn, its interval is drawn, and zero is a line they can see. It is also the honest way to show a small effect with a wide interval.

  1. Select Both on bars, with a ROPE and look right of the High dose bar.

    Expect: two slots labeled Low dose and High dose on the same category axis (the axis line, ticks and gridlines run on under them), each with a triangle, a heavy interval line and a half eye; a right-hand axis titled Difference from Control; a dotted line from the Control bar's mean across to that axis's 0.

  2. Check the triangles against the bars: trace the dotted guide from the Low dose bar's top to its triangle, and from the High dose bar's top to its triangle.

    Expect: each triangle sits exactly level with its own bar's mean. On the right axis the Low dose triangle reads about +1.5 and the High dose triangle about +4.2 (grams above Control).

  3. Hover each triangle.

    Expect: a tooltip such as High dose minus Control: difference 4.15, 95% CI [1.6, 6.7] (Welch's t, df = ...), then the ROPE reading, then "The shape is the sampling distribution of the difference."

  4. Open the Σ panel and compare the Compare-pairs rows for Control vs Low dose and Control vs High dose with the tooltips.

    Expect: the same numbers with the sign flipped (the table reads Control minus the other; the plot reads the other minus Control).

  5. Compare the High dose interval with the sampling distribution beside it.

    Expect: the interval's ends sit where the shape has thinned, exactly as the cat's eye does on a mean: the interval is cut from that shape.

  6. Click a slot to open the Difference plot panel. Use its title-bar eye to hide the plot, then restore it from the toolbar Visibility panel; try the trash and re-add it from the + menu (Difference plot).

    Expect: with the plot hidden or removed the bars widen to take the space back; re-adding draws it again with the settings it had.

Tier 4

Reading and tuning the Difference plot

What it means

The panel's first three chips change what the plot reports (they wear the blue "Changes" band): the reference category, the axis's units, and the ROPE. The rest change how it looks: the shape and its width, the marker, the interval's thickness, color and opacity.

Why use it

Units matter most. In raw units the axis speaks the outcome's language (grams). In SD units every difference is divided by the pooled within-group SD, which is Cohen's d: the same scale across studies and measures, at the cost of the concrete units. Both are honest; pick the one the audience needs.

  1. In the Difference plot panel, set Compare with to High dose.

    Expect: the slots become Control and Low dose, the axis is retitled Difference from High dose, its zero moves level with the High dose bar, and both triangles now read negative.

  2. Set it back to Control. Open Units and choose SD units.

    Expect: the triangles do not move; the axis relabels in steps of the pooled SD (the High dose difference of about 4.2 g reads about 1.4 SD, a large effect by the usual rough guide) and the title says (SD units). The tooltip adds the SD-unit value.

  3. Back to Raw units. Open Shape: try Eye, None, then Half eye; drag the width slider.

    Expect: Eye mirrors the shape around the interval; None leaves marker and interval alone; the width follows the slider. The value axis makes room for the shapes when they are on.

  4. Open Marker and cycle triangle, circle, square, diamond; open Interval and change its width.

    Expect: the marker shape changes on both slots (it keeps its dark outline so it never reads as a fourth mean); the interval line and its caps thicken together.

  5. Open Color: turn off Follow graph colors and pick one color; then Opacity.

    Expect: by default each slot wears its category's color; a custom color paints every slot the same; opacity applies to the shapes.

  6. Use the title bar's reset, then press Undo in the toolbar a few times.

    Expect: reset returns every chip to its default (Control, raw units, half eye, triangle); each Undo steps back one change, including reference and unit switches.

  7. Drag the chart wider and narrower with its corner grip; then try the chart with only two categories (click the Low dose bar and hide it with its panel's eye).

    Expect: the slots keep the category pitch as the chart resizes; with a category hidden its slot disappears and the remaining slot keeps the pitch; unhiding restores it.

Tier 4, dot and line charts

Dot charts: the axis follows the data, the summary beside the points

What it means

A bar's length is its value, so a bar chart keeps its zero baseline even with the Difference plot on. A dot or line chart encodes position, not length, so while the Difference plot is on its value axis follows the data (with a little padding) and the differences get the room they deserve. "Summary beside the points" moves each mean, its interval and its shape to the right of the raw points, the layout esci uses, so the shape never hides the data.

Why use it

Small differences on a tall axis anchored at zero shrink to nothing; letting the axis follow the data is what makes an estimation plot readable. Showing the raw points beside the summary is the honest default of the estimation tradition: the reader sees the data the estimate came from.

  1. Select Difference plot (beside) with a ROPE.

    Expect: a dot chart with the raw points showing; the value axis starts near the lowest weight (about 18 g), not at 0; each mean marker, its interval and its shape sit to the right of that category's points.

  2. Trace the dotted guides.

    Expect: each guide starts at the moved mean marker (not at the points' center) and runs to its triangle in the difference slots.

  3. Click a raw point to open the Data points panel, open its Jitter strip and untick Summary beside the points; then tick it again.

    Expect: unticked, the means return to the center of their points; ticked, they move about a third of the slot to the right. Undo covers the switch.

  4. Switch the chart type to Bar and back to Dot.

    Expect: as a bar chart the value axis returns to a zero baseline (bars keep their zero); as a dot chart it follows the data again. The Difference plot survives the switch.

  5. Set a manual axis minimum (click the value axis, Range, Min) below the data and then clear it.

    Expect: a manual range wins over the follow-the-data rule while it is set; clearing it hands the axis back.

Tier 5

The ROPE (region of practical equivalence)

What it means

A ROPE is the band of differences you declare too small to matter, decided before looking at the result and stated in the outcome's units (here, grams). The plot draws it as a gray band across the difference slots and reads every interval against it: wholly inside means the difference is negligible by your definition; wholly outside means it is too large to be negligible; an interval that overlaps the band leaves the question open, usually for want of data.

Why use it

"Different from zero" is rarely the question. "Big enough to matter" is, and a ROPE is how you answer it without hand-waving. It also makes equivalence claims possible: an interval wholly inside the ROPE is positive evidence of no meaningful difference, something a non-significant p value can never give you. The verdict depends on the bounds, so state them with the result.

  1. On Both on bars, with a ROPE, find the gray band in the difference slots.

    Expect: a band from 1 g below the Control mean to 1 g above it (the demo's ROPE is -1 to 1), spanning from the first slot to the difference axis, labeled ROPE. The right axis carries a tick beyond each edge of the band, so the axis reads across it.

  2. Hover both triangles and read the ROPE clause.

    Expect: Low dose ends with Against the ROPE [-1, 1]: overlaps it. (its interval reaches into and beyond the band) and High dose with outside it. (its whole interval clears the band).

  3. Click a slot, open the ROPE chip.

    Expect: From -1 to 1, a Clear button, one verdict line per comparison ("Low dose vs Control: overlaps the ROPE", "High dose vs Control: outside the ROPE") and a short explanation. The strip wears the blue "Changes: what counts as negligible" band.

  4. Change the bounds to -5 and 5 (press Tab or Enter after each).

    Expect: the band grows to cover 10 g; the verdict lines and tooltips change (Low dose now reads inside the ROPE, High dose overlaps it); the value axis makes room if a bound reaches past the data.

  5. Type the bounds the wrong way round (2 then -2), then an equal pair (1 and 1).

    Expect: a reversed pair is swapped into order and applied; an equal pair is ignored (the previous ROPE stays until the bounds differ). Use Clear to remove a ROPE.

  6. Open the Σ panel, Compare pairs.

    Expect: a ROPE column after 95% CI with inside / outside / overlaps per row; the header and each verdict have popovers; each row's APA copy ends with, for example, ..., outside the ROPE [-5, 5].

  7. Close the Statistics panel, click a slot, press Clear in the ROPE strip and reopen the Statistics panel. Then set the bounds again, remove the Difference plot with its title-bar trash, and look at the Statistics panel once more.

    Expect: Clear removes the band, the tooltip clause and the ROPE column. With the plot removed the column goes too (the ROPE lives with the plot; hiding the plot with its eye keeps the column, since the setting is still in force). Adding the plot back from the + menu brings the band and the column back with the bounds you set.

  8. Export the chart (toolbar download button, PNG or SVG) with a ROPE set.

    Expect: the difference slots, the band and its label are in the exported image exactly as on screen; the panel is not.

Tier 6

A Group By variable

What it means

With a Color / group variable, each group is compared within itself: every category minus the reference category, for that group's own cells. The markers dodge inside each slot exactly as the group's bars dodge in their category, and each marker still sits at its group's own category mean. Because each group has its own reference mean, each group gets its own dotted zero guide and its own difference axis, drawn in the group's color and tagged with its name.

Why use it

The two-factor design (treatment by sex, drug by genotype) is the one most experiments hit first. Comparing within each group keeps the question simple ("what did the dose do in females? in males?") and keeps the marker-at-its-own-mean identity intact, which is what lets a reader check the plot against the bars by eye.

  1. Select Grouped by sex, with a ROPE.

    Expect: dodged F and M bars per treatment, a legend, and in each of the two difference slots two triangles, F left of M, offset exactly like the bars. Two dotted zero guides in the F and M colors leave the two Control bars. Two right-hand axes, tagged F and M in their colors, and one title Difference from Control.

  2. Read the F triangles against the F axis and the M triangles against the M axis.

    Expect: each axis's zero sits level with its own Control bar's mean; each triangle sits level with its own bar (Low dose F triangle = Low dose F bar top, and so on). The two axes have the same tick spacing, offset vertically by the two reference means.

  3. Hover the four triangles.

    Expect: tooltips begin with the group, F: Low dose minus Control: ..., M: High dose minus Control: ..., each with its own Welch test, interval and ROPE reading.

  4. Find the ROPE bands.

    Expect: one gray band under each triangle (four in all), each as wide as the F-to-M pitch and placed at its own group's reference mean, so the F and M bands sit at different heights. The ROPE label sits under the first slot's bands.

  5. Click a slot; in the panel set Compare with to High dose, then back; open the ROPE chip.

    Expect: both groups re-derive against their own High dose cell (both zero guides and both axes move); the reference note mentions that each group is compared within itself; the ROPE verdict lines are prefixed with the group (F: Low dose vs Control: ...).

  6. Hide one sex (Alt+click the other sex's legend swatch to solo it, or hide the group from the toolbar Visibility panel), then show it again.

    Expect: with one group hidden the plot falls back to one marker per slot, one zero guide and one axis; restoring the group brings the second set back.

  7. Open the Σ panel's Compare pairs with Compare set to Both (the default) and find the rows the plot draws.

    Expect: rows such as F: Control vs Low dose match the F triangles (sign flipped), and the M rows match the M triangles; the ROPE column agrees with the tooltips.

  8. Switch the chart type to Dot with data points on, and try the Data points panel's Summary beside the points.

    Expect: the group markers keep their spread inside each slot (the line family's marker spread), the guides start at each group's mean marker, and the beside-the-points nudge applies per cell.

Repeated Measures

Repeated Measures (paired data)

What it means

On Repeated Measures the difference is each subject's change averaged, and its interval comes from the paired t test, so it is usually far narrower than the two occasions' error bars suggest. The sampling distributions on the occasions use each occasion's own SE; the Difference plot uses the paired SE. With a between-groups variable, each group gets its own paired comparison, zero guide and axis, exactly as in the previous section.

Why use it

This is where estimation plots shine: two occasions can overlap heavily while every subject improved, and only the paired difference shows it. Drawing that difference with its own interval is the honest picture of a within-subjects effect.

The demo has no paired data, so load this small set (ten subjects, two occasions, two groups). Copy it, open Load data in the app, and paste it into the box.

  1. Start a new chart with the Repeated Measures analysis; put before and after in Repeated measures. Add Sampling distribution and Difference plot from the + menu.

    Expect: two bars (before, after) with shapes; one difference slot, after, whose triangle sits at the after mean, a narrow interval, and a tooltip that names the paired t with df = 9.

  2. Open the Σ panel and compare the before vs after row with the tooltip.

    Expect: the same paired difference and interval (sign flipped: the row reads before minus after).

  3. Put group in Between-groups variable.

    Expect: two bars per occasion; the difference slot holds an A and a B triangle; two zero guides, two tagged axes; tooltips A: after minus before: ... (paired t, df = 4).

  4. Add Panels (any variable) and check the + menu.

    Expect: the Difference plot is not drawn on a faceted chart and the + menu does not offer it (panels are a follow-up). Remove the Panels variable and the plot returns with its settings.

Around the chart

Help, glossary, export and undo

What it means

Every new part is a first-class citizen of the chart: it has a name in Label parts, an entry in the glossary, it rides exports and copies, and every change is one Undo away.

Why use it

A student who meets a cat's eye for the first time can click it and read what it is without leaving the chart.

  1. On Both on bars, with a ROPE, open ? in the toolbar and choose Label parts.

    Expect: among the chips, one points at a sampling distribution and one at the Difference plot, each with a plain explanation on hover and a click that opens its panel. Esc clears them.

  2. In the same help panel open Glossary and search ROPE, cat's eye, estimation plot and mean difference.

    Expect: one entry each (ROPE, Sampling distribution (cat's eye), Difference plot (estimation plot), Mean difference), each with a definition, "How to read it" and "Common misread".

  3. Open ?, Check graph.

    Expect: no warning about the Difference plot or the ROPE (they are not rule violations); the usual checks run.

  4. Export the chart as PNG and as SVG (toolbar download button) and open both.

    Expect: shapes, slots, guides, axes and the ROPE band appear in every export exactly as on screen.

  5. Save the project (.pand), reload the page, and open it again.

    Expect: every setting comes back: the overlays, the reference, the units, the ROPE bounds, the marker and shape choices.

  6. Make five quick changes across these panels and press Undo five times, then Redo.

    Expect: each press reverts exactly one change, instantly, including turning an overlay on or off.

Reference

Words to know

Mean difference (Δ)
The first mean minus the second, in the units of the value axis. The estimate itself, before any standardizing.
Confidence interval
The range of true differences the data are consistent with. A 95% interval built from the same t test as the p value excludes 0 exactly when that test is significant at .05.
Sampling distribution of a mean
The t distribution of the sample mean scaled by its standard error: how plausible each value is for the population mean. Drawn as the cat's eye.
Sampling distribution of a difference
The same idea for a difference between means, with the difference's own SE and degrees of freedom (Welch's for two independent groups, n - 1 for paired data). The shape in each difference slot.
Difference axis
The right-hand axis of the Difference plot. Its zero sits level with the reference mean, so a marker at a category's own mean reads as that category's difference from the reference.
Reference category
The category every other category is compared with (Compare with). Changing it changes every difference shown.
SD units
Differences divided by the pooled within-group standard deviation of the cells shown: Cohen's d. Comparable across measures; abstract in exchange.
ROPE
Region of practical equivalence: the differences small enough to call negligible, decided in advance, in the outcome's units. Intervals are read as inside, outside or overlapping it.
Welch's t
The two-group t test that does not assume equal variances; the default behind every Compare Groups difference here (fractional degrees of freedom are normal).
Paired t
The t test on each subject's own change; behind every Repeated Measures difference here.
